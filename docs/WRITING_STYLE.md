# Brandon Keao Website Writing Style

**Status:** Working draft for review  
**Applies to:** Public website copy on the `website-v2` branch  
**Last updated:** 2026-08-23

## Purpose

Write like a peer who has done the work. Tell the reader what Brandon believes, what he has seen, how sure he is, what he does not know, and what should happen next. Keep the original website's human texture. Do not sand it down into consultant copy.

This guide adapts the July 2026 Rebrandly `Brandon Communication Style Master`, the EPD document guidelines, the recovered Manager README, Brandon's older blog corpus, and the January website voice work. The Rebrandly files were marked tentative and were written for workplace communication, so their evidence informs this guide without making every rule universal.

## The voice

- **Builder first.** Start from making, shipping, fixing, and learning.
- **Peer energy.** Sound like an experienced colleague, not an analyst narrating a framework.
- **Calibrated confidence.** State what is known plainly. Name uncertainty instead of hiding it.
- **Concrete systems thinking.** Explain how customers, products, teams, information, and decisions connect.
- **Useful honesty.** Include constraints, tradeoffs, and what Brandon is still working on.
- **Service to the team.** Leadership is about creating clarity, removing obstacles, and helping other people lead.

## Website register

The public website sits between Brandon's conversational and executive registers:

- concise enough for scanning, but not reduced to slogans;
- warm enough to feel personal, but not chatty or performative;
- confident enough for senior leadership, but never inflated;
- specific enough to be credible, with important claims connected to evidence;
- comfortable using an occasional fragment or emphatic line when it sounds genuinely Brandon.

## Page rules

### Homepage

- Lead with the durable promise, not a framework name.
- Use recognizable customer, product, or organizational problems.
- Prefer plain verbs: build, understand, decide, test, fix, lead.
- Pair every major claim with proof, a source note, or an explicit review gate.

### About

- Tell one coherent story: engineering and services roots, product leadership, systems thinking, current building.
- AI is part of the current chapter. It is not the explanation for the entire career.
- Personal detail earns its place by revealing values or constraints.

### Services

- Describe the reader's situation, the work Brandon will do, and the outcome they should expect.
- Avoid inflated category language. Name the engagement plainly.
- Keep current price and availability claims gated until Brandon confirms them.

### Manager README

- Preserve the original directness and quirks.
- Prefer factual updates over stylistic cleanup.
- Do not convert personal values into generic leadership principles.

### Case studies

- Problem, Brandon's role, what changed, and proof.
- Numbers must be sourced, derived, explicitly deferred, or removed.
- Separate team outcomes from Brandon's individual contribution.
- Name what remains unknown.

## Sentence and structure patterns

- Lead with the conclusion or conviction. Explain it next.
- Use short paragraphs, usually one to three sentences.
- Vary the rhythm. A short line can carry conviction when it earns it.
- Prefer periods and colons. Use semicolons when two thoughts genuinely belong together.
- Use em dashes sparingly; never let them become the page's dominant cadence.
- Use first person for beliefs and direct experience. Use second person when explaining how working together will feel.
- Use headings that make a claim or answer a reader's question.

## Language to preserve

These phrases are strong source material, subject to factual review where needed:

- "get unstuck and build momentum"
- "capture customer value"
- "surprise and delight users"
- "leadership comes from everywhere"
- "starting is the best way to begin learning and make progress"
- "I see things as systems"
- "the compounding awesomeness of continually fixing small things"
- "I serve you, not the other way around"
- "I care about things that actually work"

## Avoid

- self-narrating analysis: "what this shows is," "the key insight," "this reframes";
- consultant fog: leverage, synergy, unlock, transformative, holistic, best-in-class;
- abstract labels when a concrete mechanism will do;
- unsupported certainty or polished claims without evidence;
- uniform three-item lists and identical sentence pacing;
- generic leadership language that could appear on anyone's website;
- AI vocabulary as decoration;
- excessive process terms such as framework, guardrail, operating model, or ecosystem when a plain noun works;
- hollow enthusiasm, urgency, and sales pressure.

## Evidence and identity gates

- Nothing is published merely because it is in this branch.
- Testimonials, metrics, employer claims, client stories, pricing, availability, and personal facts require Brandon's review.
- When a source is only a summary, do not present reconstructed sentences as quotations.
- When this guide conflicts with Brandon's direct edit, Brandon's edit wins and the guide should learn from it.

## Quick review card

Before copy is ready for Brandon:

1. Does it sound like a person who did the work?
2. Is the main point clear in the first few lines?
3. Are the nouns and verbs concrete?
4. Does every important claim have evidence or a visible review gate?
5. Did we preserve useful roughness instead of polishing away personality?
6. Would Brandon plausibly say this out loud?
